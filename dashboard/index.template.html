The one idea behind every number on this site

Why these numbers

Ordering too little and ordering too much are not equally bad, and which one is worse decides how much you should order. That's the whole thing.

If you order too little

You lose the profit on a sale you could have made. Annoying — but you still have your money, and the goods you didn't sell are still yours to sell tomorrow.

If you order too much

You lose what you paid for everything that doesn't sell. For fresh food that's most of it. This is usually the more expensive mistake, and it's the one people make by trying to never run out.

So the right amount to order isn't "enough to never run out"

If wasting a unit costs you more than missing a sale, then running out sometimes is the cheaper outcome — and the calculator deliberately leaves you short on the quietest days, because covering those days would cost more in waste than it earns in sales. That's why the recommended plan on the next tab runs out 48% of the time and still costs 29% less than what most stores do.

This isn't a shortcut or an approximation. It's a standard result in operations research (the "newsvendor" problem), and the maths is a single line: order enough to cover demand P of the time, where P = your missed-sale cost divided by the sum of both costs. Put your two numbers in the calculator and it does exactly that.

What the calculator does with your data

It looks at how much you actually sold, day by day, and builds up a picture of how much your demand swings around — not just the average, but the good days and the quiet ones. Then it picks the order quantity that leaves you covered often enough to be worth it, given your two costs. It doesn't assume your sales follow a neat curve, which matters for slow-moving items where most days are zero: those get handled correctly rather than being smoothed into a fractional daily average that never happens in real life.

What it needs from you: at least a few weeks of daily sales per product. More is better — a couple of months gives a much steadier read on your busy days. It doesn't need prices, promotions, or anything else, though it will use a price column if you have one.

Why the best plan still runs out of stock sometimes

The best plan runs out of stock about 48% of the time. That's not a mistake — it's the cheaper choice. When throwing away a wasted item costs more than losing a sale, it's cheaper to run out sometimes than to always have enough on the shelf. These numbers depend on a few assumptions you can change

The sales data doesn't say what your profit margin is, how much of your unsold stock actually spoils, or what it costs to hold it. This page assumes a 30% profit margin, that 60% of unsold stock is a total loss, and a small daily holding cost — reasonable numbers for fresh food, but you should use your own. Why the answer usually isn't "always keep it in stock"

Missing a sale costs you the profit on that one item — you still have the goods, you just didn't sell them today. Wasting stock costs you almost what you paid for it, because most of it gets thrown out. When wasting stock costs roughly three times more than missing a sale, the cheapest plan runs out of stock well below the "always in-stock" target that feels safe by default. It would have been easy to only prove this works on your best-selling items — they're the easiest to predict, since they sell every day. For anyone curious how this was built and tested

Technical details

Nothing on this page is a black box. This section shows the model comparisons, the testing method, and every real bug we caught while building it — written honestly, including the parts that didn't work at first. Skip it if you just wanted the numbers; they're on the earlier tabs.

How the predictions were tested

Every model was tested the same way real forecasting works: trained only on data up to a cutoff date, then checked against sales that happened after that date — never on data it had already seen. This was repeated 5 times at different cutoff dates, and we report the average and the range across those 5 tests. A model that wins on average but does badly in one test is a different, riskier bet than one that wins consistently — averages alone hide that difference.

A baseline beat the model, and it is reported rather than tuned away

LightGBM wins overall by 0.005 MASE — comfortably inside a fold-to-fold spread of 0.018–0.026. On the sparse band AutoETS wins outright (0.9931 vs 1.0090), and on fold 0 it beats the GBM head to head. What the GBM actually buys is not a large accuracy jump: it is one model instead of 720, a quantile distribution the classical methods don't provide, and SHAP attributions the optimizer can be defended with.

MinT reconciliation — coherence, and what it costs

Forecast each level independently and the numbers don't add up: SKU forecasts don't sum to store forecasts. MinT fixes that exactly — and the bottom level pays for it.

Which forecast feeds which consumer

Reconciliation improves every aggregate level and makes the bottom level ~1.5% worse on MASE. Orders are placed at item × store, so the cost function optimises against the unreconciled forecast — coherence with a store total buys nothing when deciding how many units of one SKU go on one shelf. The reconciled forecasts serve the aggregate planning views instead, where coherence is the requirement. USE_RECONCILED = False is a named constant pinned by a test, not a default.

Five real catches · the part that can't be faked

Every real bug we caught along the way

Every one of these produced plausible-looking output. That's what makes them worth writing down honestly: none of them announced itself, and four of the five were caught by asking a number to justify itself, not by anything crashing.

01

784 rows where 7 belong Phase 8 · /optimize

The optimize endpoint fetched every horizon for a SKU with no date filter, pulling 28 days × 7 quantile levels × 4 stores into a single array and interpolating across it. The output looked entirely reasonable — order quantities in a sane range — over a demand distribution that was structurally meaningless: duplicated quantile keys from different days, sorted by value with no coherent ordering. Nothing failed. It was caught by checking the row count, seeing 784 where one coherent curve needs 7, and asking why. Fixed by pinning to a single period, which is the classic newsvendor framing anyway.

02

A 500 where a 422 belonged Phase 8 · /optimize

A caller-chosen Cu/Co can legitimately push the critical ratio past the top fitted quantile — Cu ≫ Co drives it toward 1.0. The interpolator correctly refuses to extrapolate a tail the model never estimated, but the handler didn't catch that, so a valid request returned a bare server error. The happy-path test at a moderate ratio could never have reached it; the test that found it was written specifically to probe the edge.

03

Serving quantiles that cross Phase 8 · /forecast

LightGBM fits each quantile level as an independent model, and nothing constrains them to be ordered — roughly 1.3% of rows come out with q0.90 above q0.95, squarely inside the band the newsvendor rule selects from. A crossing there returns a lower stocking quantity for a higher service level: silently wrong, in the direction that causes stockouts. The read-time fix existed already; the endpoint simply forgot to apply it. The fixture now plants a deliberately crossed grid so it can't regress quietly.

04

A DELETE that kept widening Phases 5–9 · scorers

Three scorers write to the same metrics table, and each clears its previous output first. The point scorer originally deleted everything, wiping the quantile scorer's rows depending on call order. Narrowing it to "everything except pinball" fixed that symptom but not the cause — it still swept up the reconciliation metrics and the WRMSSE figure, so retraining the model for a wider quantile grid silently destroyed Phase 6's results. This dashboard's MinT panel would have been built on an empty table. Found while extracting data for this page: two queries came back with 1 row and 0 rows where 8 and 2 were expected. The real fix, applied to all three: delete an allowlist of what you write, never a denylist of what you recognise — a denylist can always grow to cover someone else's rows.

05

A quantile grid that couldn't express its own premise Phase 7

The brief specified quantiles at {0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99}. But the whole point of the project is that perishables have a low optimal service level — and realistic fresh-food critical ratios land between 0.25 and 0.63, below the lowest fitted level. Every order would have been clamped at the median, and the headline finding would have been invisible: not wrong, just unreachable. Caught by computing realistic critical ratios before wiring the optimizer rather than by noticing clamped output afterwards. The grid was extended to seven levels and the model refit.

What the pattern says

Four of five were found by interrogating a number that looked fine — a row count, a metric that came back thin, a ratio computed ahead of the code that would consume it. None was found by a crash. That is the argument for building the checks that catch structure rather than exceptions: a perturbation test that corrupts the future and asserts the past didn't move, a scorer that reports how many series it excluded, a coherence gap asserted to floating-point zero rather than printed and admired.
