Tier 1 · the reasoning

Why a full shelf is the expensive mistake

Two mistakes are possible when you order. You can order too little and miss a sale, or order too much and throw stock away. They do not cost the same, and the whole method is arithmetic on that difference.

Order too little
You lose the margin

A customer wanted it, you did not have it. On a 30% margin you are out 30% of the price. The stock itself cost you nothing, because you never bought it.

Order too much
You lose most of the unit

It went unsold and it will not keep. You paid 70% of the price for it and threw away most of that, plus the cost of holding it while it sat there.

Fig. 01 — the ratio, worked out once

Divide the cost of ordering too little by the two costs added together, and you get the fraction of the time it is worth being in stock. That fraction is the in-stock target. You do not choose it; the two costs do.

missed sale  = 30% of price  (the margin)
thrown away  = 70% of price × (60% + 2%)  = 43.4%
in-stock target  = 30 ÷ (30 + 43.4)  = 40.9%

Those three percentages are assumptions. They are yours to change on the How much to order tab, and every figure downstream moves when you do. What is not an assumption is the division.

Fig. 02 — what that means on the shelf

A 40.9% target means you expect to run out on more days than you have stock left over. That reads like a failure and is not. Running out costs you 30%. Being left with it costs you 43.4%. If you cannot have both, take the cheaper mistake more often.

This is the part of the method that survives being explained to the person doing the ordering, which is why it is here and not in a footnote. Nothing after it is more sophisticated than a division.

Fig. 03 — where the forecast comes in

The target says which point of the demand spread to order to. The forecast supplies the spread — not one number, but seven, from a quiet day to a very busy one. The order lands where the target falls inside that spread.

Which is why a better forecast is worth money here in a way it is not worth money in a report: a tighter spread at the same target means less stock for the same protection.

Fig. 04 — what would make this wrong

Overlapping windows

The 28-day totals share days, so they are correlated and the tails are slightly tighter than independent sampling would give. Both tiers have this property.

One dataset, one category

This is M5 food sales in California. The method is general; this evidence is not. Your own data is the only test that counts for you.

Costs are assumed

Margin, spoilage and holding were chosen, not measured. They set the size of the saving. They do not set its sign.
