Daily sales and what comes next

The one idea behind every number on this site

Why these numbers

Ordering too little and ordering too much are not equally bad, and which one is worse decides how much you should order. That's the whole thing.

If you order too little

You lose the profit on a sale you could have made — your margin, so a 30% margin on a $6 item costs you $1.80 and on a $1 item costs 30¢. Annoying, but you still have your money, and the goods you didn't sell are still yours to sell tomorrow.

If you order too much

You lose what you paid for everything that doesn't sell — not the shelf price, the wholesale cost, because you never earned the margin. For fresh food you lose nearly all of it; where you can discount it, less. This is usually the more expensive mistake, and it's the one people make by trying to never run out.

So the right amount to order isn't "enough to never run out"

If wasting a unit costs you more than missing a sale, then running out sometimes is the cheaper outcome — and the calculator deliberately leaves you short on the quietest days, because covering those days would cost more in waste than it earns in sales. That's why the recommended plan on the next tab runs out 48% of the time and still costs 29% less than what most stores do.

This isn't a shortcut or an approximation. It's a standard result in operations research (the "newsvendor" problem), and the maths is a single line: order enough to cover demand P of the time, where P = your missed-sale cost divided by the sum of both costs. Move the two sliders in the calculator and it does exactly that.

Why the two settings are percentages, not amounts

A missed sale on a $6 bottle and a missed sale on a $1 loaf are not the same loss, so one flat cash figure can't price a mixed product list. The calculator asks for two rates instead and applies them to each product's own price, taken from the unit_price column of your file:

  • Profit margin — the share of the shelf price you keep. Run out, and that's what the missed sale costs you: price × margin.
  • Written off if it doesn't sell — the share of what you paid that you can't recover. Note it comes off your cost, not the shelf price: you never earned the margin, so you can't lose it twice. That works out to price × (1 − margin) × write-off.

These are the same two formulas the engine behind the rest of this site uses on the Walmart data, so the calculator and the results on the other tabs are costed identically rather than by two different rules.

One consequence worth knowing, because it looks like a bug and isn't: the service level the two settings imply is the same for every product. Price appears in both costs, so it cancels out of the ratio between them. What is not the same for every product is the money — a write-off on an expensive item costs more — or the quantity, since each product's order is a percentile of its own sales history. Two products at an identical 41% service level routinely order very different amounts.

What the calculator does with your data

It looks at how much you actually sold, day by day, and builds up a picture of how much your demand swings around — not just the average, but the good days and the quiet ones. Then it picks the order quantity that leaves you covered often enough to be worth it, given your two costs. It doesn't assume your sales follow a neat curve, which matters for slow-moving items where most days are zero: those get handled correctly rather than being smoothed into a fractional daily average that never happens in real life.

What it needs from you: at least a few weeks of daily sales per product, and a unit_price column. More history is better — a couple of months gives a much steadier read on your busy days. Without a price a product still gets an order quantity, but its money figures fall back to a default price and the results table marks it with an asterisk so you know which ones those are.

Why the best plan still runs out of stock sometimes

The best plan runs out of stock about 48% of the time. That's not a mistake — it's the cheaper choice. When throwing away a wasted item costs more than losing a sale, it's cheaper to run out sometimes than to always have enough on the shelf. Today's approach runs out less often (31%) — but that lower number is bought with far more waste: 78,106 units thrown out instead of 20,714. A low "in-stock rate" sounds good but isn't the same as low cost — check the total-cost column, not the stockout column alone.

These numbers depend on a few assumptions you can change

The sales data doesn't say what your profit margin is, how much of your unsold stock actually spoils, or what it costs to hold it. This page assumes a 30% profit margin, that 60% of unsold stock is a total loss, and a small daily holding cost — reasonable numbers for fresh food, but you should use your own. Go to How much to order and move the sliders: the smarter plan keeps winning across the whole range, from barely-perishable to everything-spoils.

Why the answer usually isn't "always keep it in stock"

Missing a sale costs you the profit on that one item — you still have the goods, you just didn't sell them today. Wasting stock costs you almost what you paid for it, because most of it gets thrown out. When wasting stock costs roughly three times more than missing a sale, the cheapest plan runs out of stock well below the "always in-stock" target that feels safe by default. Drag the wasted-stock slider down toward zero and the recommended target climbs back up toward 95% — that's the only situation where "always keep it in stock" is actually the cheapest plan.
